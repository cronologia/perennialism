Perennialism — Cronologia

Chronology of the Traditionalist School as an intellectual movement (1921–present)

An open, source-referenced chronology of perennialism — the Traditionalist School of René Guénon, Ananda Coomaraswamy and Frithjof Schuon — as a movement of ideas: the books, the journals, the political adaptations and the reception, from the 1920s to the school's unexpected afterlife in 21st-century politics. The Maryamiyya order's initiatic history is the sibling project cronologia/tariqa.

About

This is a work-in-progress dataset about a contested intellectual lineage whose political receptions are actively disputed. Every fact is cited; dates the sources dispute are flagged with a ? mark; and every characterization — above all about the Evola line, the alt-right reception, contested affiliations (Eliade) and living figures — is attributed to the scholar or actor making it, never asserted in the site's own voice. Corrections against published sources are welcome.

What it is
The Traditionalist School: the current of thought founded by René Guénon and developed by Ananda Coomaraswamy and Frithjof Schuon, holding that all orthodox religions transmit one primordial, perennial truth — and that modernity is the story of its loss.[1]
Founding corpus
Guénon's books of 1921–1945, from the 'Introduction générale' to 'The Reign of Quantity'; Coomaraswamy's metaphysical essays after 1932; Schuon's 'The Transcendent Unity of Religions' (1948).[3] [4] [6]
House journal
Études Traditionnelles (Paris, 1936–1992) — the renamed Le Voile d'Isis (founded 1889), dominated by Guénon and his school for over half a century.[18] [19]
The two lines
Schuon's religious-metaphysical line (which organized as the Maryamiyya order — see the sibling tariqa project) and Julius Evola's political-heroic adaptation, whose postwar radical-right reception runs to Dugin, the alt-right and the Bannon episode — every link in that chain attributed.[5] [21]
The name's traps
'Perennial philosophy' predates and exceeds the school: Steuco coined it in 1540, Leibniz reused it, neo-Scholastics applied it to Thomism, and Aldous Huxley's 1945 bestseller made it mean a universalist mysticism the Guénonians rejected. This site is about the Guénonian school; the others appear only for disambiguation.[2] [43]
Scholarship
Mark Sedgwick's 'Against the Modern World' (2004) and 'Traditionalism' (2023) are the academic backbone; Wouter Hanegraaff situates the school critically within the study of Western esotericism; Benjamin Teitelbaum's 'War for Eternity' (2020) covers the political chapter — with reviewers disputing how coherent that network really is.[20] [21] [26] [46]

Chronology

Key events in chronological order. A ? flag marks dates not yet verified against a primary source.

YearDatePlaceEvent
1540s
1540 1540 Italy Steuco coins 'philosophia perennis' — Agostino Steuco's 'De perenni philosophia' introduces the term — for a continuity thesis inside Christian Platonism, centuries before and apart from the Guénonian school.[43] [2]
1920s
1921 1921 Paris, France Guénon's first books — 'Introduction générale à l'étude des doctrines hindoues' frames 'Tradition' against modernity; 'Le Théosophisme' separates the school-to-be from occultism and Theosophy.[3]
1923 1923 Paris, France 'L'Erreur spirite' — Guénon's companion polemic against Spiritism completes the boundary-drawing against the occult milieu.[3] [42]
1924 1924 Paris, France 'Orient et Occident' — Guénon's program for a Western elite reoriented by Eastern metaphysics — and his break with the Catholic intellectuals among whom he had first written.[3]
1925 1925 Paris, France 'L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vêdânta' — Guénon's central metaphysical exposition, published in 1925 by Bossard (Paris) — confirmed against the French Wikipedia bibliographic record and Zoccatelli's CESNUR study, which both date the first edition to 1925; the '1927' of a derived source was an error.[37] [38] [3]
1925 1925/1927 Paris, France The Regnabit episode — Regnabit — revue universelle du Sacré-Cœur, founded in June 1921 by Fr. Félix Anizan OMI, carries nineteen Guénon articles between August/September 1925 and May 1927, the symbolist scholar Louis Charbonneau-Lassay having recruited him — the high point of his attempted rapprochement with Catholic milieux. Guénon's 12-page letter of 8 June 1928 to Charbonneau-Lassay closes the episode; Guénon blamed 'the hostility of some neo-scholastic milieus' (his framing), while Zoccatelli's study documents intra-congregational controversies around Anizan's Sacred Heart theology from 1923.[37] [41] [42]
1927 1927 Paris, France 'La Crise du monde moderne' — The school's most-read statement of civilizational critique — the template for all its later 'crisis' literature.[3]
1929 1929 Paris, France 'Autorité spirituelle et pouvoir temporel' — Guénon asserts the priority of the sacerdotal over the royal — the doctrinal fault line that would divide him from Evola.[3]
1930s
1930 1930 Cairo, Egypt Guénon settles in Cairo — He writes thereafter as a Muslim while addressing a mainly French readership, until his death in 1951.[3]
1932 1932 Boston, USA Coomaraswamy's metaphysical turn — The Boston Museum of Fine Arts curator shifts from art history to studies of tradition, myth and metaphysics — the essays of 1932–1947 make him the school's comparativist of 'traditional art', Vedanta and Platonism.[6] [33]
1934 1934 Milan, Italy Evola's 'Revolt Against the Modern World' — Julius Evola's political-heroic adaptation of Traditionalism subordinates the priestly to the regal — against Guénon's 1929 position; Guénon's review rejected key theses while granting the work's interest.[5] [44]
1936 1936-01 Paris, France 'Études Traditionnelles' is born — Le Voile d'Isis (founded 1889) is renamed Études Traditionnelles, reflecting Guénon's dominance of the review — the school's house journal until 1992, with a wartime gap 1940–45.[18] [19]
1939 1939 London, England 'Peaks and Lamas' — Marco Pallis's best-selling mountaineering-and-Buddhism book carries a Traditionalist reading of Tibet to a wide audience.[8]
1940s
1940 1940-05 England 'Look to the Land' coins 'organic farming' — Lord Northbourne — later a Traditionalist author and translator of Guénon — introduces the term 'organic farming', framing agriculture as a clash of worldviews; the school's most unexpected export.[32] [22]
1945 1945 Paris, France 'The Reign of Quantity' — Guénon's summa on modernity as the reign of quantity is published in the Liberation moment.[3]
1945 1945 New York, USA Huxley's other 'Perennial Philosophy' — Aldous Huxley's bestselling anthology (Harper & Brothers, New York, 1945; Chatto & Windus, London, 1946) popularizes the phrase in a universalist-mystical sense — indifferent to orthodoxy and initiation — that the Guénonians rejected as syncretism. Same words, different lineage.[7] [2]
1947 1947-09-09 Needham, Massachusetts, USA Death of Ananda Coomaraswamy[6]
1948 1948 Paris, France 'The Transcendent Unity of Religions' — Schuon's classic statement of the transcendent unity of religions (French 'De l'unité transcendante des religions', 1948; English translation by Peter Townsend, Faber and Faber, 1953 — not 1957); T. S. Eliot: 'I have met with no more impressive work in the comparative study of Oriental and Occidental religion.'[4]
1950s
1951 1951-01-07 Cairo, Egypt Death of René Guénon[3]
1951 1951-11-20 Rome, Italy Evola tried and acquitted — Arrested as alleged ideologue of the neofascist FAR, Evola declares himself 'superfascista' while denying party fascism, and is acquitted — the trial that fixes his postwar political persona.[5]
1958 1958 Paris / Lausanne 'Sacred Art in East and West' — Titus Burckhardt's canonical Traditionalist theory of sacred art across five traditions (English translation by Lord Northbourne).[9]
1958 1958 United States 'The Religions of Man' — Huston Smith's textbook (later 'The World's Religions') dominates American comparative-religion teaching; his openly perennialist 'Forgotten Truth' (1976) follows under Schuon's influence.[10] [34]
1960s
1961 1953/1961 Rome, Italy Evola's postwar canon — 'Men Among the Ruins' (1953) and 'Ride the Tiger' (1961) join 'Revolt' as the canon of the postwar Italian radical right, of which scholarship describes Evola as chief ideologue.[5] [27]
1961 1961 Paris, France 'Understanding Islam' — Schuon's most popular book (French 'Comprendre l'Islam', Gallimard, 1961; English 1963) becomes a key channel of perennialism into Islamic studies. The 1961 first edition is confirmed by the official Schuon bibliography and Gallimard.[39] [4]
1970s
1975 1975 London / Paris 'Logic and Transcendence' — Schuon's fullest engagement with modern philosophy (French 'Logique et transcendance', Les Éditions Traditionnelles, 1970; English 1975). The 1970 French first edition is confirmed by the official Schuon bibliography.[39] [4]
1980s
1980 1980 London, England 'Temenos' launches — Kathleen Raine, Keith Critchlow, Brian Keeble and Philip Sherrard found the review devoted to 'the arts of the imagination' — seed of the Temenos Academy (1990), of which the Prince of Wales (now King Charles III) has been patron since its founding.[15] [30]
1981 1981 Edinburgh, Scotland Nasr's Gifford Lectures — Seyyed Hossein Nasr delivers the Gifford Lectures, published as 'Knowledge and the Sacred' — per the Gifford records, the first Muslim and first non-Westerner to do so: the school's academic high-water mark.[28] [29]
1983 1983 London, England Lings's 'Muhammad' — Martin Lings's prize-winning biography becomes the school's biggest crossover into mainstream Islamic readership.[12]
1990s
1992 1992-12 Paris, France 'Études Traditionnelles' ends — The school's flagship journal closes with the October–December 1992 issue, after a 1936–1992 run.[18]
2000s
2004 2004 Oxford, England 'Against the Modern World' — Mark Sedgwick publishes the first full history of the movement — the academic backbone of this site.[20]
2009 2009 Moscow, Russia Dugin's 'Fourth Political Theory' — Aleksandr Dugin's post-liberal ideology (English translation 2012) draws explicitly on Traditionalism, making him the key conduit of its vocabulary into Russian geopolitics — an Evolian-line reception, attributed as such.[13]
2010s
2010 2010 London, England 'Harmony' and the royal reception — The Prince of Wales publishes 'Harmony: A New Way of Looking at Our World'; commentators — Sedgwick, Commonweal — read Traditionalist and Nasrian influence in it, though the book cites no Traditionalist sources. Reported strictly as attributed reception, never as affiliation.[14] [48]
2012 2012 Amsterdam / Cambridge Hanegraaff's 'Esotericism and the Academy' — Wouter Hanegraaff's study treats Traditionalism critically as 'religionism' within the academic history of Western esotericism.[26]
2017 2017-02-10 Washington / New York, USA The Bannon–Evola episode — After Steve Bannon's 2014 Vatican-conference remarks referencing Dugin and 'what's called the traditionalist movement', the New York Times' 'Steve Bannon Cited Italian Thinker Who Inspired Fascists' makes Evola and Traditionalism briefly front-page news, amid the alt-right republication of Evola.[45] [23]
2020s
2020 2020 New York, USA 'War for Eternity' — Benjamin Teitelbaum's ethnographic account links Bannon, Dugin and Olavo de Carvalho through Traditionalism, based on extensive Bannon interviews; reviewers dispute how coherent or consequential the 'network' really is — the book's stronger claims are the author's, not consensus.[46] [47]
2023 2023 Oxford, England Sedgwick's 'Traditionalism' — The analytic synthesis of the philosophy of Guénon, Evola and Schuon and its applications — the school historicized by the scholar who mapped it.[21]

The political adaptation — a separate line (Evola)

Evola's political-heroic reading subordinated the priestly to the regal, against Guénon's 1929 priority of the sacerdotal; Guénon's own reviews rejected key theses. Its postwar radical-right receptions are attributed to the scholar or actor asserting them — never to the Guénonian school itself.[5] [21]

  • Julius Evola 'Revolt Against the Modern World', 1934[5] [27]

A name collision — not this lineage (Huxley)

Aldous Huxley's 'The Perennial Philosophy' (1945) popularized the phrase in an experiential, universalist-mystical sense — indifferent to orthodoxy and initiation — that the Guénonians rejected as syncretism. Same words, unrelated lineage; placed here only to mark the boundary.[7] [2]

Key figures

René Guénon

1886–1951 · France / Egypt

Founder-metaphysician of the school; his 1921–1951 corpus defines 'Tradition', the critique of modernity and the primacy of metaphysics; Paris, then Cairo from 1930.[3]

Ananda K. Coomaraswamy

1877–1947 · Ceylon / UK / USA

Ceylonese-British art historian and Boston MFA curator; after his 1932 metaphysical turn, the school's comparativist of traditional art, Vedanta and Platonism.[6]

Frithjof Schuon (as author)

1907–1998 · Switzerland / USA

The school's most systematic expositor: 'The Transcendent Unity of Religions' (1948) and some twenty further books on the religio perennis. His order, the Maryamiyya, is the sibling tariqa project's subject.[4]

Julius Evola

1898–1974 · Italy

The political adaptation: warrior-regal Traditionalism ('Revolt Against the Modern World', 1934), against Guénon's priority of the sacerdotal; never a fascist party member, self-styled 'superfascista' at his 1951 trial (acquitted); chief ideologue of the postwar Italian radical right per scholarship, revived by the 2010s alt-right.[5] [27]

Seyyed Hossein Nasr

b. 1933 · Iran / USA

The academic institutionalizer: Gifford Lectures 1981 ('Knowledge and the Sacred') — first Muslim Gifford lecturer per the Gifford records — 'sacred science' and Islamic environmentalism.[11] [28]

Martin Lings (as author)

1909–2005 · England

Keeper of Oriental manuscripts at the British Museum/Library; the prize-winning 'Muhammad' (1983) and Shakespeare-as-sacred-art studies.[12]

Titus Burckhardt

1908–1984 · Switzerland

Theorist of sacred art ('Sacred Art in East and West', 1958; 'Art of Islam', 1976); Fez and Chartres monographs; UNESCO consultant on Fez.[9]

Huston Smith

1919–2016 · USA

American comparative religion's public face ('The Religions of Man', 1958); openly perennialist from 'Forgotten Truth' (1976) under Schuon's influence — the bridge into US classrooms.[10] [34]

Marco Pallis

1895–1989 · England

Mountaineer-author ('Peaks and Lamas', 1939); Tibetan Buddhism's Traditionalist interpreter and Guénon translator.[8]

Lord Northbourne (Walter James)

1896–1982 · England

Agronomist peer who coined 'organic farming' ('Look to the Land', 1940) and later translated Guénon and Burckhardt — the school's link to the organic movement.[32] [22]

Jean-Louis Michon

1924–2013 · France / Switzerland

French scholar-translator of Moroccan Sufism and Islamic art; UNESCO cultural-heritage work in Morocco; perennialist author.[1]

Aldous Huxley

1894–1963 · England / USA

Contrast figure: his 'The Perennial Philosophy' (1945) popularized the phrase in a universalist-mystical sense unconnected to — and rejected by — the Guénonian school.[7]

Mircea Eliade

1907–1986 · Romania / USA

The contested case: he read Guénon and Evola in interwar Romania; Sedgwick treats him as a concealed 'soft' Traditionalist, while Davide Marino (Aries, 2022) and the earlier Italian reassessment call the affinity a myth — the site records the debate, not a verdict.[35] [24] [36]

Mark Sedgwick & Wouter Hanegraaff

b. 1960 / b. 1961 · UK-Denmark / Netherlands

The historians of the school: Sedgwick (Aarhus; 'Against the Modern World' 2004, 'Traditionalism' 2023, the Traditionalists blog) and Hanegraaff (Amsterdam; Traditionalism as 'religionism' within esotericism studies).[20] [26]

Related organizations

Études Traditionnelles (journal)

Founded 1936 · Paris, France

The school's house journal — Le Voile d'Isis (1889) renamed under Guénon's dominance; monthly then quarterly, with a 1940–45 wartime gap, until its 1992 close. Digitized runs survive at IAPSOP.[18] [19]

http://iapsop.com/archive/materials/etudes_traditionnelles/

Sophia Perennis (publisher)

New York State, USA

James R. Wetmore's press — publisher of the English 'Collected Works of René Guénon' (23 volumes); described on Sedgwick's blog as the leading US Guénonian-Traditionalist publisher. Founding year still to pin down.[25]

World Wisdom (publisher)

Founded 1980 · Bloomington, Indiana, USA

The 'Library of Perennial Philosophy' — main English-language publisher of Schuon, Burckhardt, Pallis and Nasr; participant-run, labeled sympathetic. Shared with the tariqa project's story.[16]

Fons Vitae (publisher)

Louisville, Kentucky, USA

Aisha Gray Henry's nonprofit press — Sufism, the Ghazali series, Lings's works. The founding year remains genuinely unresolved across sources: Wikipedia's narrative gives 1984 (tracing Gray Henry's publishing back through the Quinta Essentia imprint she ran at the Islamic Texts Society, later transferred to Fons Vitae), its infobox gives 1996, and IRS/nonprofit records for 'Fons Vitae of Kentucky' give a formation year of 2000. The Quinta Essentia predecessor explains much of the split between the earlier and later dates.[31] [17] [40]

Founding year unresolved: 1984 (Wikipedia narrative / Quinta Essentia lineage), 1996 (Wikipedia infobox), 2000 (Kentucky nonprofit formation).

Temenos Academy

Founded 1990 · London, England

Grew from the Temenos review (1980, Kathleen Raine and colleagues); initially housed in the Prince of Wales's Institute of Architecture, with Charles — now King Charles III — as patron since its founding. The institutional face of the school's arts-and-imagination wing; royal 'sympathies' beyond the documented patronage are reported only as attributed commentary.[15] [30] [48]

https://www.temenosacademy.org/

Foundation for Traditional Studies / journal Sophia

Founded 1984 · Washington, DC, USA

Nasr's foundation and journal — the school's academic-facing organ (shared with the tariqa project's story).[11]

Disambiguation & nuance

The word 'perennialism' is a collision zone, and the school's political receptions are contested. These cards keep the lines straight; every characterization is attributed.

Guénonian Traditionalism ≠ Huxley's perennialism

Huxley's 1945 anthology is experiential-mystical universalism, indifferent to orthodoxy and initiation; the Guénonians insist truth is transmitted only through orthodox revealed traditions and generally rejected Huxley-style syncretism. Same phrase, two lineages.[7] [2]

≠ 'philosophia perennis' in academic philosophy

Steuco (1540) and Leibniz used the term for a continuity thesis inside Western philosophy and Christian Platonism, and neo-Scholastics applied it to Thomism — neither is the Guénonian doctrine.[43] [2]

The school ≠ the order

This site covers the school of ideas — books, journals, reception. The initiatic and organizational history of Schuon's Maryamiyya order is the sibling cronologia/tariqa project; events belong to exactly one of the two.[1]

Evola's line ≠ Schuon's line

Evola politicized Tradition — regal, activist, with a postwar neofascist reception running to Dugin and the alt-right; Schuon's line is religious-metaphysical and non-political. Guénon himself criticized Evola's inversions. Every claim placing a modern actor in this lineage is attributed to the scholar or actor making it.[5] [21] [45]

Eliade is a debate, not a datum

Mark Sedgwick reads Mircea Eliade as a concealed 'soft' Traditionalist ('Against the Modern World', 2004) — a reading he restated in 2022 even while granting that Eliade's references to Guénon grew critical after the 1950s ('his early soft Traditionalism is still found in his later work'). Against this, Davide Marino ('Mircea Eliade and René Guénon', Aries, 2022) argues the 'myth of affinity' arose from misunderstandings and that Traditionalism and Eliade's hermeneutics are theoretically irreconcilable; the earlier Italian reassessment gathered in Arcella, Pisi and Scagno's 'Confronto con Mircea Eliade' (Jaca Book, 1998) had already questioned the traditionalist reading. The site records the debate, not a verdict.[35] [24] [36]

The royal reception is attributed commentary

King Charles III's Temenos patronage (documented since 1990) and 'Harmony' (2010) are read by commentators as showing Traditionalist and Nasrian influence; the book cites no Traditionalist sources and no membership or self-identification exists. Sympathies are reported as the commentators' claims — never as affiliation.[48] [14] [30]
